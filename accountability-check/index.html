Accountability Check

Rules are meant to hold everyone. This page checks whether they do.

Ask a rule nine questions

Pick any law. We ask it nine plain questions, like who wrote it, who pays, and whether you can say no. 86,078 rules are ready to ask.

Open the nine questions

Rules or suggestions?

Think of a classroom rule: “No running.” For some kids it ends with “or you sit out.” For some it ends with “please try.” And some kids carry a note that says they can’t be sent to sit out at all.

We read about 1,380,000 sentences of law and sorted them the same way, to see who gets which.

Four kinds of sentence

MUST

You have to.

“If the enrollee accepts the offer of assistance, the plan must provide the assistance.”

42 CFR part 422

OR ELSE

You have to, or you get in trouble.

“Any person who willfully violates this section shall be fined not more than $10,000 or imprisoned for not more than one year, or both.”

29 U.S.C. ch. 11, subch. IV

PLEASE TRY

You should. Nothing happens if you don’t.

“The State agency should supply employers with a sufficient number of posters for distribution throughout their places of business and should see that the posters are conspicuously displayed at all times.”

20 CFR part 614

CAN’T BE BLAMED

Even if it goes wrong, you can’t get in trouble.

“The Federal Government shall not be liable for any claim based upon the exercise or performance of or the failure to exercise or perform a discretionary function or duty …”

42 U.S.C. ch. 68, subch. III

Most punishments in the law are written against “any person.” Almost none name the government as the one punished.

  • The fine in the middle is the same $1,000 for everyone. A thousand dollars does not weigh the same on a person as on a company.
  • “Money only, no crime” means a civil penalty: the one punished pays and gets no criminal record.
  • About two in three OR ELSE sentences give no number. They say “as provided in” another section, and the number is there.

How sure are we?

A program did the sorting, so we checked it. We pulled sentences at random and had each one read by itself. This is how often the program had put the OR ELSE on the right one.

Anyone
58 of 60 right
A business
33 of 50 right
A person
33 of 50 right
The government
27 of 50 right
  • “Anyone” is solid. The other three are right only about half to two-thirds of the time, so their numbers are rough.
  • The first check caught the program reading “an officer of a corporation” as a public officer. We fixed that and checked again. The government count fell to a third of what it was.
  • The main finding rests on the solid one: about four in five OR ELSE sentences are written against “any person.”

What this cannot see

  • In law, “any person” includes companies. So the OR ELSE against “anyone” is not only against people.
  • A MUST in one sentence can have its OR ELSE three sections later. The program reads one sentence at a time and would count that MUST as having none.
  • It cannot tell whether an OR ELSE is ever used. A penalty on paper and a penalty in life are different things.
  • It finds words, not meaning. Amounts written out in words, like “five hundred dollars,” were not read.
  • What was read: the United States Code, federal regulations, and the state codes on the check. 74,621 pieces of law, on 2026-11-17. Card agreements and the rules of a faith were not read for this.
  • Nothing here says a law is good or bad, or that anyone broke one.
