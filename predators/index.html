Predatory traits and behaviors

What published sources record about those who prey on other people: how they choose, how they get close, how they keep control, and what they do when challenged. People and institutions both.

Each one is here so it can be recognised. Open any line for what it looks like, who does it to whom, and the source in its own words.

Beside them is what studies record of animals that hunt, trick, live off or harm other animals, so the two can be held next to each other and weighed.

Our two nearest relatives

Chimpanzees and bonobos are the two living animals closest to humans. Among chimpanzees, males rule. Among bonobos, females hold the higher rank. Researchers have watched both in the wild for more than fifty years. This is what they recorded.

Chimpanzees kill each other. Bonobos almost never do.Wild studyFifty years of records from 18 chimpanzee groups and 4 bonobo groups: 152 killings by chimpanzees, one suspected killing by bonobos. Males were 92 in every 100 attackers.
The source’s words
“Our data include 152 killings (n = 58 observed, 41 inferred, and 53 suspected killings) by chimpanzees in 15 communities and one suspected killing by bonobos.”
Source
Wilson and others, Lethal aggression in Pan is better explained by adaptive strategies than human impacts, Nature, 2014. Open the source
Male chimpanzees force femalesWild studyTen years of records show males attacking the females most able to have young. Seventeen years of records with family testing show high-ranking males who frightened females over a long time fathered more young.
The source’s words
“male aggression is targeted towards the most fecund females, is associated with high male mating success and is costly for the victims.”
Source
Muller and others, Male coercion and the costs of promiscuous mating for female chimpanzees, Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 2007. Open the source
The source’s words
“long-term patterns of intimidation allow high-ranking males to increase their reproductive success”
Source
Feldblum and others, Sexually coercive male chimpanzees sire more offspring, Current Biology, 2014. Open the source
Bonobo females hold power by standing togetherWild studyThirty years of records from 6 bonobo groups. Males are bigger, yet females rank higher. Females rank highest where they band together most, and 85 in every 100 of their bands were against a male.
The source’s words
“females target males in 85% of their coalitions and that females occupy higher ranks compared to males when they form more frequent coalitions.”
Source
Surbeck and others, Drivers of female power in bonobos, Communications Biology, 2025. Open the source
Bonobos are not simply peacefulWild studyBonobo males fight each other more often than chimpanzee males do. What differs: they attack females less, females push back more, they do not gang up, and no killing has been reported.
The source’s words
“bonobos exhibited lower rates of male-female aggression and higher rates of female-male aggression than chimpanzees. Surprisingly, we found higher rates of male-male aggression among bonobos than chimpanzees”
Source
Mouginot and others, Differences in expression of male aggression between wild bonobos and chimpanzees, Current Biology, 2024. Open the source
Did people or captivity cause the violence?TestedThis is often said. The 2014 study tested it in wild chimpanzees. Groups that people had fed or disturbed more did not kill more.
The source’s words
“Variation in killing rates was unrelated to measures of human impacts.”
Source
Wilson and others, Nature, 2014. Open the source
  • These are studies of apes. They do not show why people harm each other.
  • Each study was read through its summary, not the whole paper.
